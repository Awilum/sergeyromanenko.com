I bought the motorcycle in 2025, shortly before my birthday. It was my first motorcycle in Vietnam and has become my favourite motorcycle here. Since purchasing it, I have travelled more than **37,000 kilometres** on it.

My longest journey was a trip from Da Nang to southern Vietnam and back — **7,740 kilometres over 138 days**. That journey is documented in the photographic project [From Da Nang to South and Back](https://sergeyromanenko.com/photography/from-danang-to-south-and-back/) .

The motorcycle was originally completely stock. I gradually modified and improved it based on my actual travel experience. My objective was not to build a powerful adventure motorcycle. I wanted a compact, practical, relatively lightweight mini Explorer Bike that would allow me to explore Vietnam at my own pace.

03 · A Small Bike for Big Adventures

## Why the Yamaha PG-1.

Its relatively small engine, compact dimensions, and manageable weight make it practical
in Vietnamese cities and capable on asphalt, gravel, rural roads, and light dirt tracks.

The Yamaha PG-1 is not a large touring motorcycle or a full-size off-road adventure bike. It is a 113.7 cc underbone — light, narrow, and built for daily commuting. On paper, those numbers suggest limitations. In practice, they are exactly what makes it useful for the kind of travel I do.

In Vietnamese traffic, the PG-1's width and low weight mean I can filter through gaps that would trap a larger bike. Parking is never a negotiation. Fuel consumption stays low even when carrying luggage. The automatic centrifugal clutch means no clutch lever to manage in stop-and-go city traffic or on steep, technical mountain roads.

On asphalt it is stable and predictable. On gravel and rural roads, the long-travel suspension (for this class) and 16-inch wheels absorb more than expected. On light dirt tracks, the low centre of gravity and narrow tyres let it go places a heavier bike would struggle. It is not an enduro machine — deep sand, heavy mud, and large rocks are beyond it — but for the mixed surfaces that connect Vietnam's smaller towns and mountain passes, it is surprisingly capable.

The PG-1 also has a strong aftermarket and modification culture in Vietnam. Vietnamese mechanics, metalworking specialists, and welding workshops can fabricate custom brackets, racks, guards, and mounting systems at a fraction of the cost of imported adventure gear. That ecosystem is what made my Explorer build possible.

The central message is simple: the PG-1 is an enjoyable and practical companion for slower, more exploratory journeys. It does not replace a large adventure bike. It offers something different — flexibility, economy, and the freedom to take the smaller road without hesitation.

04 · From Stock to My Own Explorer

## The evolution from factory standard to personal Explorer build.

I purchased the motorcycle in its original factory configuration and gradually transformed
it. Every modification came from a real problem discovered during the 7,740-kilometre
journey and subsequent riding.

When I collected the motorcycle, it was standard in every respect: stock tyres, stock sprockets, stock seat, stock lighting, no luggage provisions. The first kilometres felt fine around Da Nang. The problems appeared only when I left the city — punctures on rough roads, gearing too tall for mountain passes with luggage, a seat that punished hours in the saddle, lighting that disappeared on unlit highways, and no way to carry the gear a multi-day journey requires.

I did not modify everything at once. Each change was a response to a specific situation: a tyre that failed, a hill that bogged the engine, a night ride where I could not see, a sore back after 400 kilometres. The current configuration is the result of multiple modifications, experiments, improvements, and a few failed solutions.

Vietnamese motorcycle mechanics and welding specialists were essential to this process. Custom brackets, reinforced mounting points, fabricated luggage racks — these are not catalogue items. They were made in small workshops by people who understand metal and motorcycles. The ability to walk into a welding shop with a sketch and leave with a finished part the same day is one of Vietnam's hidden advantages for motorcycle travellers.

It is worth noting that a Yamaha PG-1 can be adapted to different personal styles. Some owners build street-inspired machines with lowered suspension and cast wheels. Others go mini-enduro with knobby tyres, high pipes, and enduro bars. My direction is the third: a mini Explorer / travel configuration — practical luggage, protective guards, auxiliary lighting, comfortable ergonomics, and the reliability to keep moving day after day.

These modifications are my personal choices. They are not endorsed by Yamaha Motor Vietnam or Yamaha Motor Co., Ltd., and they may affect the motorcycle's warranty, handling, or legal compliance. Every rider must make their own assessments.

05 · The Modifications

## Technical details, practical reasoning, and photographs of each change.

Numbered editorial structure — each modification documented with its purpose,
specification, and real-world result.

### 05.1 · Kenda Dual-Sport Tyres

Tyres are one of the first important upgrades after purchasing a motorcycle for mixed road conditions. The factory tyres are biased toward urban asphalt; they offer little grip on loose surfaces and puncture easily on sharp gravel.

My current setup uses **Kenda dual-sport tyres** (K270A) with a tread pattern designed for a split between paved and unpaved roads: enough siping for wet asphalt, enough block depth for gravel and dirt.

My experience spans more than 37,000 kilometres of overall riding: asphalt highways, mountain passes, gravel rural roads, rough dirt tracks, and varying weather conditions. I have not experienced a puncture with this tyre setup during the use I described.

This is not a claim that punctures are impossible or that this tyre setup is universally ideal. It is a report of my actual result. For my riding style — mixed surfaces, moderate speeds, loaded touring — they have been a practical and reliable choice.

- **Current tyres:** Kenda dual-sport

### 05.2 · Sprockets and Heavy-Duty Chain

The factory secondary ratio on the PG-1 is 3.077:1 (13-tooth front sprocket, 40-tooth rear sprocket; Yamaha specifies 3.077, or 40/13, in its documentation). That gearing favours fuel economy and top speed on flat roads. With luggage and on steep mountain roads, the engine spends too much time below its power band. I changed the final drive ratio to address this.

My current setup keeps the factory **13-tooth front sprocket** and fits a **42-tooth Super Heavy Duty rear sprocket** (factory: 40 teeth), paired with a **heavy-duty drive chain**. The final-drive ratio rises from 3.077:1 to approximately 3.23:1 (42/13).

The practical effect: improved low-speed response, noticeably better hill climbing with luggage, and reduced engine strain on sustained gradients. The trade-offs are higher engine RPM at a given road speed, a lower indicated top speed, and slightly increased fuel consumption. The heavy-duty chain handles the increased torque load; it does not increase the motorcycle's payload capacity — that is determined by the frame, suspension, and tyres.

- **Front sprocket:** 13 teeth (factory)
- **Rear sprocket:** 42 teeth, Super Heavy Duty (factory: 40 teeth)
- **Chain:** Heavy-duty drive chain
- **Final drive ratio:** ≈ 3.23:1 (42/13, vs factory 3.077:1, 40/13)

### 05.3 · Custom Protection and Lighting

I started with standard front protective guards — the generic steel loops sold as "crash bars" in Vietnamese accessory shops. They offered basic tip-over protection but no mounting points for luggage or auxiliary lights. I subsequently redesigned the arrangement for my own purposes.

The modified protective guards now integrate **custom mounting points for side luggage**, allowing the MotoWolf bags to attach securely without stressing the bodywork. Two **MT10 Ultra V2 auxiliary lights** are mounted on dedicated brackets, providing a significant increase in night visibility. The installed units offer both white and yellow lighting modes — yellow cuts through fog and heavy rain better, white provides maximum distance on clear nights.

I also replaced the main headlight with an LED unit featuring a **yellow Angel Eye–style halo**. This serves both a practical purpose (a distinctive daytime running light signature) and a visual one — the motorcycle has developed a more distinctive Explorer appearance that signals its purpose at a glance.

- **Auxiliary lights:** 2 × MT10 Ultra V2
- **Lighting modes:** White and yellow
- **Main headlight:** LED with yellow Angel Eye halo

### 05.4 · Seat, Handlebar and Front Fender

#### Seat

I completed the 7,740-kilometre journey using the original factory seat. It is quite firm and becomes uncomfortable during extended riding — especially on consecutive 300–400 km days. After returning, I replaced it with a softer, more comfortable seat with additional padding and a shaped profile. The difference on multi-day trips is immediate.

#### Handlebar

The stock handlebar position forces a slight forward lean that fatigues the shoulders and wrists over long distances. I raised the handlebar position using a riser kit, bringing the grips up and back by approximately 30–40 mm. The more upright posture reduces upper-body fatigue and improves control on technical terrain.

#### Front fender

The standard front fender sits low and close to the tyre, which can trap mud and debris on wet dirt roads. I replaced it with a more aggressive, enduro-inspired design that sits higher and wraps further around the tyre. It better suits the appearance and practical requirements of the Explorer build — less mud buildup, more tyre clearance, and a visual balance with the raised handlebar and protective guards.

These are ergonomic and aesthetic changes. I make no claims about improved mechanical performance beyond rider comfort and confidence.

### 05.5 · CarPlay and Windscreen

Navigation in Vietnam is essential — road numbers change, signage is inconsistent, and the best routes are often the smaller ones that do not appear on paper maps. I purchased an inexpensive CarPlay-compatible motorcycle display through Lazada and connected it directly to the motorcycle's electrical system using a fused power connection.

It allows me to mirror navigation from my smartphone without constantly mounting and removing the phone. The display stays on the bike, powered by the motorcycle, and connects wirelessly or via cable when I start the engine. I consider this one of the most useful upgrades for exploring unfamiliar parts of Vietnam.

I also installed a windscreen positioned in front of the display to provide additional protection from rain, sun glare, and wind buffeting. The practical benefit is a readable screen in conditions that would otherwise make a phone mount unusable.

I do not invent the device brand, operating system, exact model, or electrical specifications here — the unit is an affordable aftermarket product, and the principle (a dedicated motorcycle display with CarPlay/Android Auto) matters more than the specific hardware.

### 05.6 · Tyre Pressure Monitoring System

I installed a small tyre pressure monitoring device with external sensors on both valves. It provides real-time tyre pressure and temperature information while riding, displayed on a compact handlebar-mounted unit.

This is useful when travelling long distances, carrying luggage, and moving between different road conditions and ambient temperatures. A slow leak, an over-inflated tyre after a heat soak, or a pressure drop from a valve issue — any of these can be caught before they become a roadside problem. On a heavily loaded motorcycle in remote areas, that early warning is valuable.

I only mention parameters confirmed by the actual device: pressure and temperature. Additional displayed values (battery voltage, altitude, etc.) are omitted unless verified.

### 05.7 · Custom Rear Luggage Rack

This is another important part of the story. I redesigned the rear mounting system multiple times. Two earlier designs failed during use — one cracked at a weld under load, another bent when the motorcycle fell on rough ground. I eventually worked with a local specialist to create a stronger, custom-welded luggage rack based on my own design.

The new solution supports a rear central top case, side luggage, and different combinations of touring equipment. It is fabricated from steel box section with reinforced gussets at the stress points, bolted to the frame and subframe at multiple locations rather than relying on the grab rail alone. The side luggage mounts are integrated into the rack structure, not bolted to the bodywork.

This experience underscores the practical value of Vietnamese metalworking and welding specialists in adapting motorcycles to personal travel needs. A skilled fabricator can analyse a failed design, understand the load paths, and produce a solution that fits the specific motorcycle and the specific rider's requirements — something no universal catalogue rack can guarantee.

06 · Luggage and Weight Distribution

## Practical packing principles from 37,000 kilometres of touring.

My current configuration: yellow Yamaha MotoWolf side luggage, expandable side bags, a
central rear top case (~45 L), and custom-welded mounting hardware.

My luggage setup has evolved through the same trial-and-error process as the mechanical modifications. The current configuration uses **yellow Yamaha MotoWolf side bags**, expandable by design, and a **central rear top case of approximately 45 litres**, all mounted on the custom-welded rack described above.

#### The main lesson: larger rear case is not always better

Do not automatically assume that a larger rear top case is better for long-distance touring. A large, heavy case positioned far behind the rear axle can affect motorcycle handling and balance — the lever arm amplifies every kilogram, making the rear feel vague in corners and the front light under acceleration.

My preference, developed from experience, is to:

- Distribute weight between the sides.
- Keep heavier items as low as practical.
- Keep heavy luggage as close to the motorcycle's centre of gravity as possible.
- Avoid unnecessarily concentrating weight at the very rear.

This is personal experience and a practical packing principle, not an absolute engineering rule. Different motorcycles, different riders, and different journeys will have different optima.

#### Payload distinction

The motorcycle's maximum permissible load (including rider, passenger, luggage and accessories) is a legal and structural limit set by the manufacturer and documented in the owner's manual. I do not quote a figure here. My luggage recommendations are about handling quality within that limit. A motorcycle can be within its rated payload and still handle poorly if the weight is badly distributed. Check the applicable figure for your model year in the owner's manual before loading heavily.

- **Side luggage:** Yellow Yamaha MotoWolf expandable side bags
- **Centre top case:** ≈ 45 litres
- **Mounting:** Custom-welded rear rack

07 · Maintenance and Lessons from the Road

## Long-distance motorcycle touring requires regular attention to maintenance.

My regular practices, personal observations, and the difference between factory guidance
and lived experience.

A small motorcycle ridden hard and far needs more frequent care than a large tourer covering the same distance at lower relative load. The PG-1's engine works at higher RPM, its suspension has less travel, and its brakes are smaller. Maintenance is not optional — it is what keeps the journey moving.

#### My regular maintenance practices

- **Engine oil:** Changed every 1,000 km
- **Oil filter:** Replaced according to Yamaha’s factory service schedule
- **Air filter:** Inspected and cleaned regularly
- **Drive chain:** Inspected, cleaned, lubricated every 500–800 km
- **Front fork oil:** Upgraded to heavier weight after ~5,000 km
- **General inspection:** Before and after every long journey

The 1,000 kilometre engine oil interval is my own practice, not Yamaha's factory service schedule. It comes from extensive riding and long-distance touring in Vietnam, often loaded, in heat and dust, with the engine working at higher RPM than a commuter cycle ever would. I change the oil every 1,000 km because that is the routine that kept this motorcycle healthy across more than 37,000 km, and through the 7,740 km journey specifically.

Do not read my 1,000 km routine as a manufacturer recommendation, and do not treat it as a substitute for the factory schedule. It is the personal maintenance habit of one rider on one intensively used motorcycle. Consult the owner's manual for the authoritative schedule.

#### Special attention: front suspension

My personal observation is that the original fork setup began to feel noticeably softer after approximately 5,000 kilometres. The damping became less controlled on repeated hits, and the fork used more of its travel on typical bumps. I eventually found that the suspension needed attention and improved fork oil to obtain the firmer feel I preferred.

This is presented as my personal observation, not as an official Yamaha maintenance interval or universal failure point. The PG-1's fork is a basic damper-rod design with limited oil volume; under sustained heavy use (loaded touring, rough roads, daily riding), the oil degrades faster than the service manual's interval might suggest.

I do not invent service intervals, oil grades, pressures, torque values, or part specifications. Where appropriate, I distinguish factory maintenance guidance from my personal modifications and preferences. Consult the owner's manual for the authoritative schedule.

#### Lessons from the road

- **Carry spares.** A master link, a spare tube, a few cable ties, and a multi-tool have saved me more than once.
- **Check chain tension daily on a long trip.** The shorter gearing and heavy loads accelerate wear.
- **Tyres wear unevenly when loaded.** Rotate or replace based on the worn edge, not just the centre tread.
- **Brake pads wear faster in the mountains.** Inspect before and after every major pass.
- **Electrical connections corrode.** Dielectric grease on every connector prevents mysterious failures.

09 · Should You Buy a Yamaha PG-1 for Travelling in Vietnam?

## An honest buying perspective from one owner, not from a dealership.

What the PG-1 does well in Vietnam, what it cannot do, which modifications matter, and who
should look at a larger engine instead.

This section is for anyone considering a Yamaha PG-1 as a touring motorcycle in Vietnam. It is based on my own experience as an owner, not on press material or dealership claims. I will not quote prices or running costs here. Yamaha Motor Vietnam publishes current retail pricing, and real-world costs vary by city and dealer.

#### What makes the PG-1 suitable for exploring Vietnam?

It is narrow, light (107 kg in standard trim), low (795 mm seat height) and easy to place in Vietnamese traffic. The automatic centrifugal clutch removes one thing to manage in stop-and-go riding and on steep, technical roads. The 16-inch wheels with block-pattern tyres cope with broken asphalt, gravel connectors and ferry ramps better than typical 17-inch road machines. Parts and labour are affordable and widely available, and local welding shops will fabricate custom racks and mounts quickly, which is what made my build possible.

#### Advantages in cities, rural areas and mountain roads

In cities, filtering, parking and low-speed balance are effortless, and fuel consumption stays modest even with luggage. In rural areas, it slips over unpaved village lanes, ferry ramps and light dirt tracks without drama. On mountain roads, it is stable at a sensible pace, the low centre of gravity helps on hairpins, and my 42-tooth rear sprocket (Section 05.2) cured the factory gearing's laziness on steep gradients with luggage. The weak points on long days are the stock seat (firm), the stock lighting (weak at night) and the rear drum brake (adequate, not generous).

#### Limitations a potential buyer should understand

The 113.7 cc engine is the defining fact. Acceleration is modest, overtaking on highways requires planning, and sustained speed above 80 km/h places the engine under constant high load. There is noticeable buzz above 60 km/h. The standard version has a front disc and a rear drum, and no ABS; the 2025 ABS versions add front-wheel ABS. The tubed tyres are cheap and repairable anywhere, which matters in rural Vietnam. The fuel tank holds 5.1 litres, so long stints between fuel stops are realistic, but range depends entirely on how you ride; I did not record a measured figure.

#### Modifications worth considering for touring

In the order I actually needed them: dual-sport tyres, shorter final-drive gearing, a softer seat, raised handlebars, auxiliary lighting for night riding, a secure custom luggage rack, and tyre pressure monitoring. Navigation (my CarPlay display) earns its keep on unfamiliar roads. Every one of these is documented in Section 05 with the real-world result. None of them are exotic: most are available through the Vietnamese aftermarket or fabricated by local workshops.

#### Is it suitable for long-distance travel?

Yes, with discipline. This page is the evidence: more than 37,000 km since 2025, including 7,740 km over 138 days from Da Nang to the southernmost point of Vietnam and back. The PG-1 rewards slow, deliberate travel and punishes neglect. Frequent oil changes, chain care and honest weight discipline are the price of admission. It will not keep up with big bikes on highways, and that is not what it is for.

#### Who will enjoy it, and who should prefer a larger engine?

Buy it if you like slower travel, smaller roads, local food stops and out-of-the-way places, and if you enjoy maintaining and customising your own motorcycle. Prefer a larger engine if you routinely ride two-up with full luggage, spend most of your days at 100 km/h and above, or want unhurried overtaking on open highways. A 150 to 250 cc machine would serve that rider better. Neither choice is wrong; they are different motorcycles for different journeys.

I still reach for this motorcycle first for going away. That is the most honest recommendation I can give.

10 · Is the Yamaha PG-1 Good for Exploring Vietnam?

## An honest assessment based on 37,000+ kilometres of personal experience.

Who may enjoy this motorcycle, and where its limitations lie.

#### Who may enjoy this motorcycle

- **People who like slower travel.** The PG-1 rewards a pace that lets you notice the landscape, the villages, and the people. It is not a motorcycle for covering ground quickly.
- **Travellers exploring smaller Vietnamese roads.** Narrow mountain passes, unpaved village lanes, ferry ramps, and tight city alleys are where this motorcycle feels at home.
- **Riders who want a compact and manageable motorcycle.** Low seat height, light weight, and automatic clutch make it accessible to a wide range of riders, including those with less off-road experience.
- **People interested in customizing and improving their own motorcycle.** The PG-1 has a huge aftermarket in Vietnam, and local fabrication makes one-off parts affordable.
- **Travellers who value flexibility over speed and engine power.** The trade-off is deliberate: less power, more options.

#### Limitations

- **Small engine capacity.** 113.7 cc means limited acceleration, limited top speed (approximately 90–95 km/h indicated in stock form, lower with my gearing), and limited overtaking ability on highways.
- **Naturally limited high-speed performance.** Sustained speeds above 80 km/h place the engine under continuous high load. This is not a highway cruiser.
- **Touring comfort depends on personal modifications.** The stock seat, handlebar, and wind protection are adequate for city commuting, not for multi-day journeys. My comfort is the result of the changes documented in Section 05.
- **It requires realistic expectations and regular maintenance.** A PG-1 ridden 400 km a day, loaded, on rough roads, is operating well beyond its design intent. It will survive — but only if you maintain it.

The overall conclusion is personal rather than promotional: after more than 37,000 kilometres, this little yellow Yamaha has become my trusted companion for exploring Vietnam. It is not the right motorcycle for everyone, and it is not the right motorcycle for every terrain. But for the way I travel — slow, curious, on the smaller roads — it is hard to imagine a better partner.

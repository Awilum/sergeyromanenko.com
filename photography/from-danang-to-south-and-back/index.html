From Da Nang to South and Back

A photographic journey across Vietnam, covering 7,700 kilometers from Da Nang to the southernmost point of the country and back.

PHOTOGRAPHY
Year

Location

Format
Photographic project

A photographic journey across Vietnam, covering 7,700 kilometers from Da Nang to the southernmost point of the country and back.

I began this journey on December 6, 2025, in Da Nang. There was no carefully planned route, no detailed itinerary, and no fixed destination waiting for me at the end of the road. I simply left from Love Pier near the Dragon Bridge in Da Nang, got on my Yamaha PG-1, and started riding south.

At that moment, I did not even know where the southernmost point of Vietnam was. I had no route and no detailed idea of what I was going to find. I only knew that I needed to go south.

Perhaps I had no other choice. Or perhaps it was an opportunity to finally see more of this wonderful country.

No Fixed Destination

The journey began without a destination, and for some time I simply followed the direction of the road.

As I travelled south, the route gradually took me through very different parts of Vietnam. I passed through Kham Duc, Kon Tum, Pleiku, Buon Ma Thuot, Da Lat, Mui Ne, Phan Thiet, Vung Tau, Saigon, My Tho, Vinh Long, Ben Tre, Can Tho, and Ca Mau, before finally reaching Mui Ca Mau, the southernmost point of the country.

Each place had its own character.

Kham Duc felt quiet and almost untouched, as if the road had only just begun to open. Kon Tum had a calm and almost spiritual atmosphere, while Pleiku felt raw and windy, surrounded by the open landscapes of the Central Highlands.

In Buon Ma Thuot, the heat, red earth, and energy of the region created a completely different atmosphere. Da Lat brought cooler air, quieter streets, and a slower rhythm, making it feel like a natural pause in the middle of the journey.

Further south, the landscape changed again. Mui Ne felt dry and coastal, with long roads and intense sunlight, while Phan Thiet offered a simpler and more local rhythm of everyday life by the sea. In Vung Tau, the city and the ocean came together in a much more dynamic landscape.

Then came Saigon, loud and alive in almost every possible way.

From there, the journey continued into the Mekong Delta.

My Tho felt like the beginning of the Mekong’s rhythm, while Vinh Long was quieter, surrounded by water and greenery. Ben Tre felt deeply connected to local life, with coconut trees and villages extending along the waterways. Can Tho was more vibrant, shaped by river culture and life on and around the water.

Further south, Ca Mau began to feel increasingly remote, as though I was approaching the edge of something much larger.

And finally, there was Mui Ca Mau, where the land seemed to disappear into the horizon.

The Southern Edge

I spent Christmas and New Year in Da Lat, surrounded by cool air and silence.

Later, I celebrated Vietnamese Tet at the very edge of the country, where the land ends and the sea begins.

The journey south was filled with long days on the road, heat, challenges, solitude, and moments of clarity. For much of the journey, it was simply me, my motorcycle, and the road ahead.

Step by step, kilometer by kilometer, I finally reached the destination I had discovered only after beginning the journey.

Mui Ca Mau — the southernmost point of Vietnam.

I reached it on my Yamaha PG-1, after travelling thousands of kilometers from Da Nang.

Then I turned north.

The Journey Back

The return became another journey entirely.

Instead of rushing back to Da Nang, I continued to move slowly through the country, staying in some places longer and passing through others without knowing exactly how long I would remain.

From Ca Mau, I travelled back through Can Tho, Vinh Long, Ben Tre, My Tho, and Go Cong, continuing to explore the Mekong Delta and its constantly changing landscape.

I crossed rivers, took ferries between islands and shores, got lost on smaller roads, and allowed the journey to develop naturally around whatever I encountered along the way.

From the Mekong Delta, I eventually reached Vung Tau by boat, before continuing once again towards Saigon.

From there, the road turned north and back into the highlands. I travelled through Bao Loc and Da Lat once again, returning to a place that had already become an important part of the journey.

I then continued through Buon Ma Thuot, where I explored Dak Lak and encountered elephants, before travelling further north through Pleiku, Kon Tum, and Mang Den.

The final part of the journey took me through Quang Ngai and Tam Ky, followed by Hoi An, and eventually back to Da Nang, the city where the entire journey had begun.

138 Days on the Road

In total, I spent 138 days on the road and travelled approximately 7,700 kilometers across Vietnam.

During those months, I experienced extreme heat in Dak Lak, cold nights in Da Lat, and the heavy tropical humidity of the Mekong Delta. The weather changed constantly, and with it the landscapes, the light, the roads, and the rhythm of everyday life.

The journey became a continuous movement between very different environments, from the mountains and highlands of Central Vietnam to the coast, from the dense streets of Saigon to the waterways of the Mekong Delta, and from remote roads to some of the country’s largest cities.

Travelling by motorcycle allowed me to experience these changes gradually. Instead of moving directly between destinations, I could watch the country transform kilometer by kilometer, stop when something caught my attention, and discover places that would otherwise have remained nothing more than names on a map.

Photographing the Journey

Throughout the expedition, I travelled with my camera and photographed the world unfolding around me.

I photographed people, landscapes, roads, architecture, villages, cities, markets, everyday life, and the countless small moments that appeared between destinations.

Some photographs were carefully considered, while others happened in a matter of seconds. A person standing beside the road, someone waiting for a ferry, a conversation outside a shop, a house hidden among the mountains, a passing face, or a particular moment of light could become a reason to stop.

The camera gave me another way to experience the journey. It encouraged me to slow down, observe what was happening around me, and preserve moments that might otherwise have disappeared almost immediately.

The journey was not only about reaching the southernmost point of Vietnam. It was about everything I encountered along the way.

More Than a Road Trip

A journey of this length is difficult to understand while you are still living inside it.

For months, the experience becomes a continuous sequence of roads, places, people, photographs, changing weather, unexpected encounters, and decisions about where to go next. Only after returning can all of these separate moments begin to form a larger picture.

Looking back, this was not simply a trip from Da Nang to Ca Mau and back.

It became a way of experiencing Vietnam slowly and from a different perspective — not through a predetermined itinerary, but through the spaces between destinations and the people encountered along the road.

The journey taught me to pay attention to what happens between the places marked on a map, because sometimes those moments become more important than the destination itself.

A Thousand Photographs

More than 1,000 photographs were created during the expedition.

Together, they document far more than the route itself. They preserve the people I encountered, the places I passed through, the landscapes that surrounded me, and the small moments that would otherwise exist only in memory.

Some photographs describe a particular location, while others are more concerned with atmosphere, light, movement, or the feeling of being there.

Taken together, they form a visual record of 138 days spent travelling through Vietnam.

The photographs are fragments of one much larger story, created gradually over thousands of kilometers.

The Book

The photographs from this expedition will become a photographic book documenting the journey from Da Nang to the southernmost point of Vietnam and back.

The book will bring together the people, landscapes, roads, cities, villages, and everyday moments that shaped these 138 days on the road.

The photographs presented on this page are only a small selection from the project. The complete collection is considerably larger, and the book will allow the journey to be experienced as a continuous visual story rather than simply as a collection of individual images.

7,700 kilometers cannot be reduced to a handful of photographs.

These images are an introduction to the journey and to the world I encountered along the way.

The larger body of work will continue to take shape as the book develops.

The Project

From Da Nang to South and Back is a photographic record of one long journey across Vietnam.

It is a project about movement, distance, observation, discovery, and the experience of leaving without knowing exactly where the road will lead.

When I left Da Nang on December 6, 2025, I did not know where the journey would take me, how long I would remain on the road, or what I would find along the way.

I eventually reached the southernmost point of Vietnam, turned north, crossed the country again, and returned to Da Nang 138 days after I had left.

I came back with 7,700 kilometers behind me and more than 1,000 photographs in my archive, along with memories and stories that could never fit into a single journey description.

It is impossible to fit everything I saw, everything I felt, and everything I experienced into one project page.

This is only the beginning of the story.

The road brought me back to Da Nang.

The photographs will take the journey further.
